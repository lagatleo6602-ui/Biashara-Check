Is it machine learning, a statistical model, or rules?

All three, each for a different job. Simple sums are never sent to a model, and the models never change your figures.

Your business figures

Worked out from the figures you enter for each month. KES throughout.

Gross profit
Total sales − cost of goods sold
Gross margin
Gross profit ÷ total sales × 100
Operating profit (profit or loss)
Gross profit − running expenses. Below zero is a loss month.
Net margin
Operating profit ÷ total sales × 100
Net cash flow (cash left over)
Money received − money paid out
Profit vs cash difference
Operating profit − net cash flow. Positive: money tied up in stock, credit sales or loan repayments.
Break-even sales
Running expenses ÷ gross margin: the sales you need to cover running costs
Margin of safety
(Total sales − break-even sales) ÷ total sales × 100
Sales received as money
Money received ÷ total sales × 100
Costs per 100 shillings of sales
(Cost of goods + running expenses) ÷ total sales × 100
Months of stock
Stock value at month end ÷ cost of goods sold
Money owed, in months of sales
Money owed ÷ total sales
Average sale
Total sales ÷ number of sales
M-Pesa share
Received by M-Pesa ÷ money received × 100

    The risk %: what it means and how it's worked out

    The risk % is the chance that at least one of your next 3 months is a loss month (operating profit below zero), based on your figures so far.

    1. Your figures become 20 signals. For your latest month: margin this month and over 3 months, change in margin, loss months among the last 3, sales and cost trends, cost and running-cost shares, cash left over, months with more cash out than in, M-Pesa share, number-of-sales trend, money owed and its change, stock on hand, how long you've kept records, time of year and type of business. Each uses only months up to now.
    2. The model compares you with thousands of past business-months whose next 3 months are known, and gives a probability between 0 and 1. We show it as a %.
    3. The % is calibrated. The models give extra weight to the rarer loss months while learning, which pushes their % up. A final correction (Platt scaling: chance = 1 ÷ (1 + e−(a × log-odds + b))), fitted on training months the model hadn't seen, brings the % back in line with how often losses really followed. It never changes which business is riskier than which.
    4. The % is put in a band: Low below 35%, Moderate from 35%, High from 60%.
    5. SHAP explains it. SHAP splits the prediction into how much each signal pushed it up or down. The three biggest "up" pushes are your main reasons; the biggest "down" pushes are what's working.

    How the model learned: each past business-month is labelled 1 if a loss followed within 3 months and 0 if not. Months whose next 3 months aren't recorded yet are never used for learning.

      The risk model hasn't been trained yet, so there are no test results to show. Until then the dashboard uses the simple rule described above.

      A fair test: predicting months it had never seen

      The model learned from months … to …, then was tested on … later business-months (… to …) from … businesses, before seeing what happened next. A 3-month gap between the two stops the answers leaking into the learning.

      When we say a %, does it come true that often?

      This is called calibration. If the % is honest, of all the months we gave about 30%, about 30 in 100 should have been followed by a loss. Bars side by side should be about the same height.

      Honest limits

      • It predicts from your records only. A road closure, a new competitor or a family emergency isn't in the figures.
      • Wrong or missing months make the check less reliable. 12 months or more with no gaps gives the best result.
      • It is decision support, not a guarantee, and it is not a credit score.

      Looking 1 to 12 months ahead (your outlook)

      On the dashboard you choose how far ahead to look, from 1 to 12 months. The outlook is the chance of at least one loss month between now and the month you chose. Looking 3 months ahead is the same question as the main risk check.

      1. One model for every horizon. Each past business-month is used once for every horizon whose months are all recorded (1 month ahead, 2 months ahead … 12), with the horizon as an extra input. One model learns them all, which uses the data far better than twelve separate models.
      2. It can only go up. A loss within 3 months is also a loss within 6, so the model is told the chance may only rise with the horizon (a monotone constraint), and the bars on the dashboard never go down.
      3. It agrees with the risk check. The whole curve is shifted (on the log-odds scale) so its 3-month point equals the main risk check: logit(ph) = logit(qh) + logit(p3, main) − logit(q3). The shift is the same for every horizon, so the order stays the same.
      4. Reasons for your choice. SHAP explains the prediction for the horizon you picked, so the reasons can differ: this month's cash matters more 1 month ahead, trends and debt more 12 months ahead.
      5. Warnings. If you choose a horizon other than 3 months, you also get an alert when that outlook goes up a band, and the monthly email includes it.

      The financial distress model (logistic regression)

      What it predicts: the chance that over the next 3 months more money goes out than comes in (a cash shortfall), or money owed rises by more than half a month's sales. A business can make a profit and still run short of cash, so this complements the loss risk.

      The equation. Each of your eight figures is standardised against the average business, z = (your value − average) ÷ spread, then:

      log-odds = b0 + b1z1 + b2z2 + … + b8z8
      chance = 1 ÷ (1 + e−log-odds)

      The weights (b) are fitted by maximum likelihood with no penalty and no class weights, so the chances aren't pushed up or down. The dashboard shows the whole sum for your business under "See the full calculation".

      The distress model hasn't been trained yet.

      The fitted weights

      Odds ratio = eb: how the odds of distress change when that figure is one spread (one standard deviation) higher. Above 1 raises the chance, below 1 lowers it. A p-value under 0.05 means the effect is very unlikely to be chance.

      Your data and privacy

      Biashara Check follows Kenya's Data Protection Act, 2019.

      What we keep

      • Your business code (like SME-001), type of business and the monthly figures you enter.
      • Your email and mobile number, encrypted, stored apart from your figures, and used only for your account, codes and the messages you switch on.
      • Your password, only as a one-way hash that nobody can read back.
      • An activity log: logins, changes to figures and admin actions, so every change can be traced.

      What we never keep

      • Your name, ID number or business name.
      • Your M-Pesa statement: it's read once for monthly totals and thrown away, with its password. No names, numbers or receipts are stored.
      • Card or bank details. Biashara Pro is paid by M-Pesa on your phone.

      Who can see it

      • You see only your own business. Other owners can never see it.
      • The study team (admins) can see figures by business code. Your email and phone are hidden; showing one is recorded in the activity log.
      • Nothing is shared with lenders, SACCOs or anyone else. Reports leave the app only when you download or email them to your own confirmed email.
      • The risk models learn patterns from all businesses together, by code only. They keep weights and patterns, not copies of anyone's monthly records.

      Your rights

      • Download all your figures as a CSV from the Account page (Delete account tab), whether or not you delete.
      • Correct any month (Edit on the dashboard).
      • Delete your account: your business, every month, your login and your contact details are erased for good.
      • Change your username and password, and switch messages off, on the Account page.

      How it's protected

      • Passwords hashed with scrypt and a random salt. Logins end after 12 hours, and changing your password logs out every other device.
      • Five wrong passwords lock that login for 10 minutes.
      • Email and phone encrypted at rest (Fernet, AES); looked up by a keyed hash.
      • One-time codes stored only as hashes, expiring after 15 minutes.

      Data integrity: keeping the figures right

      • Checks when you type: no negative amounts, no future months, one record per month, and warnings for likely typing mistakes (sales 3 times last month's, costs far above sales, M-Pesa above all money received…). You confirm before such a month is saved.
      • Checks when you upload: every row is shown to you before anything is saved, and months already saved are never overwritten unless you choose to replace them.
      • Every change is logged: each month added, edited, deleted or uploaded is written to the activity log with who and when.
      • Every prediction is stamped with the model version that made it, and kept, so past checks can be compared with what happened.
      • Fingerprint: a SHA-256 code made from all your figures. Change any figure by even one shilling and the fingerprint changes completely, so a report can be matched to the records it came from.
